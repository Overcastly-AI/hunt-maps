Ridgeline · redesign proposal · direction C of three

The Field Record Sheet 04 of 11 · Big Pine Hollow · Hocking Co., Ohio · drawn 08 Aug 2026

The thesis. This app's output is not a view, it is a decision a person acts on — they hang a stand, they walk an access route in the dark, they burn a vacation day — so the interface should carry the weight of a record rather than the lightness of an app. It is built as a quadrangle sheet: the map sits inside a neatline and every control lives in the printed collar around it, which means chrome structurally cannot occlude the map or the conditions it depends on. Paper has always had to state things that change and things it does not know, and its answers are better than ours — it dates the entry, it prints the revision in a different ink, and it rules a blank line rather than guessing. And because a survey sheet has always had to say how well it knows what it draws, the evidence grade behind every biological constant is carried the way a geologic contact carries its certainty: by line convention, not by a colour or a badge.

Woodland green #6F8C57
Semantic — held / complete. The quad's woodland tint, used as a halftone screen whose density is the coverage figure.
Photorevision purple #5E4A8C
The accent, and it has one job only: USGS overprinted photorevised detail in purple to mean newer than this sheet, not checked on the ground. That is exactly what a live value is.
Query red #B0361F
Semantic — absent, assumed, queried. Hairline marks and the query glyph only, never a filled surface; that restraint is what keeps it a correction pen instead of terracotta.

Secondary cartographic inks: water blue #4E7A93, ochre #A8842B (semantic: partial / attention). Semantic colour is deliberately separate from the accent — the accent means unverified, not bad.

Layout — the concept

A quadrangle sheet. The map is closed by a double neatline; every control lives in the collar — the printed margin — with the legend on the right edge and the conditions along the foot, where a quad puts its scale, its declination diagram and its revision statement.

The rule that falls out of it is the product argument: chrome never crosses the neatline. The 2026-08-07 audit found three separate occlusion defects — the drawer over the layer rows, the wind popover over Topo and LiDAR relief, and on mobile the layers sheet hiding the very conditions bar its own content depends on. A collar cannot produce any of them. The eight-interaction mobile walkthrough in that audit becomes three, because nothing has to be closed to read the thing it refers to.

What I changed after reviewing the plan

The first pass was cream stock, a serif display and a warm oxide accent — which is terracotta with a different name and the single most over-produced "considered, tactile" look there is. Three revisions: the stock went to a cool green-biased drafting grey and the warmth moved into the ink (contour brown over the terrain plate) and into the tooth (an inline feTurbulence noise at 5% multiply) rather than the paper colour; the accent became photorevision purple, chosen because it already means "unverified" in this trade; and the serif was demoted from display hero to legend-and-marginalia, which is where printed maps actually use one.

Two conventions the whole system rests on

Both are borrowed from print because print already solved the problem, and both carry real information — a borrowed structure that carries nothing is decoration.

1 · Evidence grade is a line convention

On a geologic map a contact is drawn solid when observed, dashed when approximate, dotted when concealed, and queried when uncertain. That is already Ridgeline's four-grade evidence scale, drawn by people who had one ink. The rule under every value carries its grade — legible with no colour, no glyph and no legend lookup, which is the only thing that survives a glove, a snow-glare screen and a red lamp at once. Colour is redundant reinforcement, never the carrier.

2 · A record handles a live value by dating it, not by refreshing it

This is the hard question the direction has to answer: paper is static and this thing is live. Paper's answer is better than a spinner. A printed sheet never claims currency, it claims provenance — it stamps every entry with when it was true, overprints anything newer than the sheet in a second ink, and rules a blank line for a field it does not hold. Notes on the direction

Which light I designed for

The night plate is the primary. A hunter is in position before shooting light; 05:30 in the dark on a phone is the moment this product is used, and the day plate is the one used at the truck and the kitchen table. Both are real — the plate is a state of the document, set by the sun and pinnable by the user, not a per-device hack. That is why the desktop frame above is the day plate at 13:40 and the phone frames are the night plate at 05:31: same sheet, same layout, same ink weights, two printings.

The night-vision problem, solved rather than ignored. A papery light interface at 05:30 costs twenty minutes of dark adaptation and it costs it to the eye that has to find a trail marker. So the night plate is the sheet as it reads under a red field lamp — a restricted long-wavelength ink set with blue absent, which is the same reason aviators and astronomers use red. No surface exceeds ~9% relative luminance; the brightest ink (#D9B48F) appears only in figures and rules, never as a filled area. Warmth here is not decorative: it is the mechanism.

The honest cost: under that lamp, photorevision purple collapses toward pink and query red toward orange. That is precisely why grade and state are carried by line convention — solid, dashed, dotted, queried — with hue as redundant reinforcement only. Nothing in this system is legible by colour alone, on either plate.

How a printed sensibility handles live and unknown

Paper never claims currency; it claims provenance. Four states, applied identically to every value in the product:

  • Fresh — value, grade rule, and a stamp naming the observation time, its age and its source.
  • Changing now — overprinted in photorevision purple, the ink USGS used for detail added from aerial photography and not field-checked. The thermal flip at 07:05 ±25 min is printed, not animated; a moving number is unreadable with a glove on and untrustworthy at any speed.
  • Stale — the value stays and its ink stays. Only the stamp changes, to ochre and to "re-observe". A record does not delete an entry because it aged.
  • Not held — a ruled blank with the unit printed beside it, plus a line saying which plates depend on it. "The field exists and is empty" is a different statement from a default, and the whole product turns on that difference.

Above them sits the revision line along the foot of the sheet, which states per input when that input was last true — the device a printed quad has always used to be honest about being out of date. The ◆ count at its right end is the number of values currently overprinted, and it is the same mark on both viewports.

What I kept from the current system, and why

  • The ConditionsBar's model, entire. The product audit rates it 9/10 and it is right: labelled cells, the bottom edge, an explicit "Not set". It is re-set in this type system and given a stamp line, but the model is untouched.
  • Its construction, which matters more than its look. Every cell here is a plain button with no explicit width inside a flex row, so painted surface and interactive surface are the same rectangle by construction — the fix QA-FIELD's Finding 1 asks for, applied to the new command row too so the defect cannot be rebuilt in a wider shape.
  • The 44 px touch floor from tokens.ts, exceeded everywhere: command cells 62 px, conditions cells 76 px, the unblock action 44 px full-width.
  • The blocked-layer reason string, verbatim. The audit calls it the best moment in the app. It is not improved, it is promoted — given a struck entry, a margin note and the action that resolves it.
  • Tokens stay the single source of truth. Everything above is a new token set in packages/design, two plates deep, with tokens.css still generated from tokens.ts so MapLibre paint properties get the same ink values the collar does.

The risk I took

Re-inking the map itself. The terrain render is neutral grey; this direction prints it in contour brown, and on the night plate in a hot oxide under a lamp pool. That is a deliberate trade of a little tonal neutrality for a document that reads as one object — and it is where the warmth of this direction lives, since the paper is a cool drafting grey rather than cream. If it goes wrong it goes wrong in the worst place: a hillshade whose ink shifts the apparent aspect of a slope is exactly the "confidently wrong about terrain" failure this project's second non-negotiable forbids. Mitigation is that the re-ink is a hue multiply at fixed opacity over an unchanged luminance channel, so relative relief is preserved and can be proven so — but it must be proven, not assumed.

The secondary risk is that the collar spends screen. On desktop the map is 57% of the sheet, less than a full-bleed map would be. I think that is the right trade — a full-bleed map that gets covered by three panels is not a bigger map — but it is a trade and the founder should be told it was made on purpose.

What I would check on a real device before trusting this

  • The night plate on a mid-range LCD phone at minimum brightness, outdoors, in real dark. Cheap panels have a black floor and a backlight bleed that a design file cannot show. If #0D0B0A glows grey the whole restricted-ink argument weakens, and the fallback is a deeper stock and heavier ink.
  • Whether the four line conventions are distinguishable at 1.5 px on a 1080p-class screen through a phone case, with a glove, at arm's length. Dotted versus dashed is the pair most at risk. Falsifiable by hit-test: show four rules, ask which is which, at 05:30, not at a desk.
  • Re-inked relief against the neutral render, on a known surface. Run the synthetic plane and hyperbolic paraboloid from the terrain validation loop through both and confirm apparent aspect and slope direction are unchanged. Screenshot review proves nothing about a hillshade.
  • Hit-testing the collar with elementFromPoint, at 390×844 as well as 1440×900 — the mobile-only gap the audit found. Specifically: the legend sheet's bottom edge must never intersect the conditions collar's rect, which is now an invariant this layout can actually be held to.
  • The tally at 30 marks, and at 300. It is honest and legible for one region; a user with a season's worth of downloaded ground will need it to collapse, and I have not designed that.
  • Paper tooth on an OLED at low brightness. A 5% noise layer can band or shimmer on some panels; if it does, it comes out — the tooth is the least load-bearing thing here.
